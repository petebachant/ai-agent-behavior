## Abstract

We report mean drag measurements on smooth, rigid, surface-piercing circular
cylinders of diameter *D* = 25.4, 38.1 and 50.8 mm, towed at speeds of
0.2–1.1 m/s through a [TODO: length] m towing tank with a submerged length of
*L* = 0.60 m. Each cylinder was towed at ten speeds, giving Reynolds numbers
4.7 × 10³ ≤ *Re* ≤ 5.0 × 10⁴ and diameter-based Froude numbers
0.28 ≤ *Fr*_D ≤ 2.2. Of the 30 runs, 29 passed a pre/post-run zero-drift check.
Using water properties at each run's measured temperature, they give a mean
drag coefficient *C*_D = 1.15 ± 0.03 (mean ± one standard deviation), and the
three diameters agree to within 0.014. The in-line force fluctuations peak at
twice the vortex-shedding frequency for a Strouhal number of 0.20 in every
run, which is consistent with regular subcritical Kármán shedding. A weak rise
of *C*_D with *Re* (about 0.06 per decade) is discussed in relation to the
free-surface and free-end effects that accompany it in this configuration.

## 1 Introduction

[TODO: motivation. Why this configuration and these measurements? E.g. teaching
or benchmark dataset, validation of a model, or a precursor to a specific study.]

The flow past a circular cylinder at subcritical Reynolds numbers
(roughly 10³ < *Re* < 2 × 10⁵) has been studied extensively. Over this range the
boundary layer separates laminar, the wake sheds vortices at a Strouhal
number near 0.2, and the drag coefficient of a nominally two-dimensional
cylinder stays roughly constant at about 1.0–1.2 (Roshko 1961; Williamson
1996; Zdravkovich 1997). [CHECK values against the chosen references.]

Practical cylinders, such as piles, risers and instrument masts, often pierce
the free surface and end in a free tip, so they are not two-dimensional. The
free surface adds wave-making and changes the near-surface wake as the
Froude number rises (Chaplin & Teigen 2003). The free end adds a tip flow that
changes the base pressure along the span. Confinement in a finite tank adds
blockage (Maskell 1963; West & Apelt 1982).

This paper presents a compact dataset of towed-cylinder drag over a
decade of *Re* for three diameters. It documents the procedure in enough detail
to be reproduced, including data quality control and the sensitivity of the
results to the processing choices. [TODO: state the specific contribution
relative to existing data. This is the part a reviewer will press on.]

### 2.2 Force measurement and calibration

The streamwise force was measured with a single-axis load cell [TODO: make,

### 2.3 Test procedure

### 2.4 Data reduction

For each run, the zero (tare) was taken as the mean voltage over *t* < 1 s,
before the carriage moved. The force record is *F*(*t*) = (*V*(*t*) − *V*₀)/*S*,
where *S* is the calibration sensitivity. The mean drag is the average of
*F*(*t*) over the steady window 3 s < *t* < 8 s (1000 samples; shaded in
Fig. 1). The force plateau is flat over this window in every accepted run,
and the 1 s block means stay within ±1 % of the window mean. [CHECK: at
0.2 m/s the window starts after only ≈ 4 *D* of travel for the 50.8 mm
cylinder. The plateau is flat, but a reviewer may ask about wake
development.]

The drag coefficient and Reynolds number are

  *C*_D = *F̄* / (½ ρ *U*² *D L*),  *Re* = *U D* / ν,

where ρ(*T*) and ν(*T*) are evaluated at the measured water temperature
(Tanaka et al. 2001 for density; a Vogel-type fit for viscosity).
[CHECK: preferred property source, e.g. IAPWS.] *U* is the nominal carriage
speed. The diameter Froude number is *Fr*_D = *U* / √(*g D*).

**Quality control.** The zero was also measured over the last second of each
record, after the carriage had stopped. In 29 runs it matched the pre-run
tare to within 4 mN. In run 17 (*D* = 50.8 mm, *U* = 0.8 m/s) the post-run
zero was offset by 4.2 N, and the force rose steadily through the steady
window instead of reaching a plateau (12.4 → 14.4 N over 3–8 s). This points
to load-cell drift during the run, which was also noted in the lab log. Run 17
was excluded. Including it would raise the 50.8 mm mean *C*_D from 1.157 to
1.177. [TODO: re-run this case if possible.]

**Fluctuation frequency.** The dominant frequency *f* of the force fluctuations
in the steady window was found from a discrete Fourier transform. This
provides an independent check on the speed, diameter and timing.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 Force records

Figure 1 shows a typical force record. After the acceleration transient the
drag reaches a steady mean with a superposed oscillation. In every accepted
run, the dominant frequency of this oscillation matches *f* = 2 St *U*/*D*
with St = 0.200 (range 0.199–0.201). This is the expected in-line force
response at twice the Kármán shedding frequency, and it confirms that the
wake shed regularly at a Strouhal number typical of the subcritical regime
(Williamson 1996; Norberg 2003). The RMS fluctuation is 4–7 % of the mean
drag.

> **Figure 1.** Streamwise force on the 25.4 mm cylinder at *U* = 0.6 m/s
> (run 5, *Re* = 1.4 × 10⁴). The shaded band is the 3–8 s averaging window.
> *File: `paper/fig_timeseries.pdf` (from `paper/make_figures.py`).*

### 3.2 Drag coefficient

Figure 2 and Table 3 show *C*_D against *Re*. The three diameters overlap
in *Re* over most of the range and collapse onto one band. The accepted runs
give *C*_D = 1.151 ± 0.030 (mean ± SD, *n* = 29), with all values between
1.09 and 1.22. The diameter means differ by less than half the run-to-run
scatter:

**Table 3.** Mean drag coefficient by diameter (accepted runs).

| *D* (mm) | *n* | *C*_D (mean ± SD) | *Re* range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25.4 | 10 | 1.143 ± 0.033 | 4.7 × 10³ – 2.6 × 10⁴ |
| 38.1 | 10 | 1.152 ± 0.026 | 6.5 × 10³ – 3.6 × 10⁴ |
| 50.8 | 9 | 1.157 ± 0.034 | 8.9 × 10³ – 5.0 × 10⁴ |
| All | 29 | 1.151 ± 0.030 | 4.7 × 10³ – 5.0 × 10⁴ |

A least-squares fit of *C*_D against log₁₀ *Re* over all accepted runs has a
slope of 0.058 ± 0.019 per decade (*r* = 0.50). The trend is clearest for the
25.4 mm cylinder (0.107 ± 0.029), weaker for 38.1 mm (0.072 ± 0.028) and
absent for 50.8 mm (−0.006 ± 0.050). The full per-run results are in

## 4 Discussion

### 4.1 Comparison with two-dimensional data

The measured *C*_D ≈ 1.15 lies near the top of the range usually quoted for
nominally two-dimensional cylinders at these Reynolds numbers
[CHECK against specific data, e.g. Wieselsberger's data as compiled in
Zdravkovich 1997]. This is somewhat surprising for a finite cylinder with a
free end. Free-end cylinders with these aspect ratios usually show a reduced
sectional drag near the tip, which lowers the overall coefficient. Several
effects could offset this, and each should be assessed:

1. **Blockage.** The cylinder frontal area is 2.3–4.5 % of the tank cross
   section. Blockage raises the measured *C*_D, so classical corrections
   (Maskell 1963; West & Apelt 1982) would reduce the values by a few percent,
   with the largest correction for the 50.8 mm cylinder. The data show no
   clear increase of *C*_D with blockage: the 50.8 mm mean exceeds the
   25.4 mm mean by only 1.2 %, which is well within the scatter.
   [TODO: choose and apply a correction, or justify omitting one.]
2. **Free-surface effects.** *Fr*_D reaches 1.6–2.2 at the highest speeds.
   At these Froude numbers the free-surface deformation around a
   surface-piercing cylinder is large, and the wave-making drag can be
   significant (Chaplin & Teigen 2003).
3. **Force on the dry part of the model.** [CHECK: does the load cell also
   sense aerodynamic drag or splash forces on the part of the model above the
   waterline? Probably negligible, but it should be stated.]
4. **Definition of *L*.** *C*_D scales with 1/*L*. The quoted submerged length
   is the still-water value. The wetted length changes at high *Fr*_D because
   of the bow wave and the trough behind the cylinder.

### 4.2 The weak Reynolds-number trend

The rise of *C*_D with *Re* seen for the two smaller cylinders cannot be
attributed to Reynolds number alone. In a towing tank both *Re* and *Fr*_D
increase with *U*, and over this dataset they are correlated (*C*_D
correlates with *Fr*_D at *r* = 0.43 and with log *Re* at *r* = 0.50).
The 50.8 mm cylinder reaches the highest *Re* at the lowest *Fr*_D and shows
no trend. This is consistent with the rise being a Froude-number (free-surface)
effect rather than a Reynolds-number effect, but the dataset is not large
enough to separate the two. [CHECK: whether to make this argument. Further runs
with *Re* and *Fr*_D varied independently, e.g. different *L* or more
diameters, would settle it.]

### 4.3 Sensitivity to processing choices

Two processing choices made during the analysis changed the results
noticeably:

- **Submerged length.** An early analysis used *L* = 0.70 m and gave
  *C*_D ≈ 0.94–1.01 for the 25.4 mm cylinder. With the correct *L* = 0.60 m
  the same data give 1.09–1.18. [CHECK: these early values are exactly the
  current ones multiplied by 0.6/0.7, which is what points to *L* = 0.70 m in
  `figures/cd_vs_re_oct.png`. Confirm before stating it in the paper, or drop
  this point.]
- **Water viscosity.** Using ν at 20 °C instead of the measured 13.7–17.6 °C
  overstates *Re* by 7–14 %. *C*_D is almost unaffected (< 0.2 %, through ρ).

## 5 Conclusions

- The mean drag coefficient of smooth surface-piercing cylinders with
  *L*/*D* = 12–24 is *C*_D = 1.15 ± 0.03 for 4.7 × 10³ ≤ *Re* ≤ 5.0 × 10⁴, and
  it is independent of diameter within the measurement scatter.
- In-line force fluctuations at twice the shedding frequency confirm regular
  vortex shedding at St = 0.20 in all runs.
- A weak increase of *C*_D with speed (≈ 0.06 per decade of *Re*) appears
  for the smaller cylinders. It is confounded with Froude number and may be a
  free-surface effect.
- Blockage (2–5 %), free-surface and free-end effects have not been corrected
  for. [TODO: update once Sect. 4.1 is resolved.]

# Reprocess all 30 tow-tank runs from raw data -> paper/results.csv
# Standard library only (no numpy/pandas) so it runs anywhere.
# Run from the project root:  python paper/reprocess.py
#
# Differences from process.py / process_b3.py / analysis.ipynb:
#   - reads raw data from data/raw (not the Dropbox path)
#   - fits both calibrations instead of hard-coding the March sensitivity
#   - rho and nu evaluated at each run's logged water temperature (not 20 C)
#   - flags runs whose post-run zero drifts from the pre-run tare
#   - estimates the dominant force-fluctuation frequency in the steady window
import csv
import glob
import math
import os
import statistics as S

G = 9.81
L = 0.60            # submerged length, m
TANK_W, TANK_H = 0.90, 0.75
T0, T1 = 3.0, 8.0   # steady window, s
DRIFT_TOL = 0.05    # N; |post-run zero - pre-run tare| above this -> excluded


def linfit(x, y):
    mx, my = S.mean(x), S.mean(y)
    sxx = sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in x)
    b = sum((a - mx) * (c - my) for a, c in zip(x, y)) / sxx
    a = my - b * mx
    res = [c - (a + b * xx) for xx, c in zip(x, y)]
    se_b = math.sqrt(sum(r * r for r in res) / (len(x) - 2) / sxx)
    return b, a, se_b, max(abs(r) for r in res)


def rho_water(T):  # Tanaka et al. (2001), kg/m^3
    return 999.97495 * (1 - (T - 3.983035) ** 2 * (T + 301.797)
                        / (522528.9 * (T + 69.34881)))


def mu_water(T):   # Vogel-type fit, Pa s
    return 2.414e-5 * 10 ** (247.8 / (T + 273.15 - 140.0))


def read_run(path):
    lines = [l.strip() for l in open(path) if l.strip() and not l.startswith("#")]
    sep = ";" if ";" in lines[0] else ","
    rows = [tuple(map(float, l.split(sep))) for l in lines[1:]]
    return [r[0] for r in rows], [r[1] for r in rows]


def dominant_freq(t, x, fmin=0.5, fmax=30.0, df=0.02):
    m = S.mean(x)
    x = [v - m for v in x]
    best = (0.0, 0.0)
    f = fmin
    while f <= fmax:
        w = 2 * math.pi * f
        c = sum(v * math.cos(w * tt) for tt, v in zip(t, x))
        s = sum(v * math.sin(w * tt) for tt, v in zip(t, x))
        p = c * c + s * s
        if p > best[1]:
            best = (f, p)
        f += df
    return best[0]


cal = {}
for name in ["2025-09", "2026-03"]:
    rows = list(csv.DictReader(open(f"data/calibration/loadcell_{name}.csv")))
    x = [float(r["mass_g"]) / 1000 * G for r in rows]
    y = [float(r["voltage_V"]) for r in rows]
    sens, off, se, maxres = linfit(x, y)
    cal[name] = sens
    print(f"cal {name}: sens = {sens:.5f} V/N (SE {se/sens*100:.2f}%), "
          f"max residual {maxres/sens*1000:.1f} mN")

log = {int(r["run_id"]): r for r in csv.DictReader(open("data/run_log.csv"))}
out = []
for path in sorted(glob.glob("data/raw/*/run_*.csv")):
    run = int(os.path.basename(path)[4:7])
    meta = log[run]
    sens = cal["2026-03"] if meta["date"] >= "2026-03-10" else cal["2025-09"]
    t, v = read_run(path)
    tare = S.mean([vv for tt, vv in zip(t, v) if tt < 1.0])
    zero_end = S.mean([vv for tt, vv in zip(t, v) if tt > t[-1] - 1.0])
    ts = [tt for tt in t if T0 < tt < T1]
    Fs = [(vv - tare) / sens for tt, vv in zip(t, v) if T0 < tt < T1]

    U = float(meta["speed_m_s"])
    D = float(meta["diameter_mm"]) / 1000
    T = float(meta["water_temp_c"])
    rho, mu = rho_water(T), mu_water(T)
    F = S.mean(Fs)
    drift = (zero_end - tare) / sens
    f_dom = dominant_freq(ts, Fs)
    out.append({

function F = towtank_proc(fname, sens)
    d = readmatrix(fname, 'NumHeaderLines', 3);
    t = d(:,1); v = d(:,2);
    tare = mean(v(t < 1));
    f = (v - tare) / sens;
    F = mean(f(t > 3 & t < 8));
end

=== ./process.py
# process tow tank runs -> mean drag force for each run
import glob
import os

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

DATA = "/Users/mkim/Dropbox/towtank/data/raw"
BATCHES = ["2025-10-14", "2025-11-03"]

cal = pd.read_csv("data/calibration/loadcell_2025-09.csv")
sens = np.polyfit(cal.mass_g / 1000 * 9.81, cal.voltage_V, 1)[0]  # V/N
print("sensitivity", sens)

rows = []
for b in BATCHES:
    for f in sorted(glob.glob(os.path.join(DATA, b, "run_*.csv"))):
        d = pd.read_csv(f, comment="#")
        tare = d.loadcell_V[d.time_s < 1.0].mean()
        F = (d.loadcell_V - tare) / sens
        steady = (d.time_s > 3) & (d.time_s < 8)
        rows.append({
            "run": int(os.path.basename(f)[4:7]),
            "F_mean": F[steady].mean(),
            "F_std": F[steady].std(),
        })

out = pd.DataFrame(rows)
out.to_csv("processed/forces.csv", index=False)
print("done", len(out))
